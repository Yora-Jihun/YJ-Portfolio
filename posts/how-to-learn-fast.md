## The big idea {-}

Most people learn slowly because they spend their time on the wrong part of the loop. They watch, read and highlight, which is input, and they rarely try something, get stuck and check the result, which is feedback.

Fast learners flip the ratio: a little input, a lot of practice, and quick feedback on every attempt. Everything below is a way of doing that.

## Start with something you want to make

A syllabus tells you what exists. A project tells you what you need. Pick something small and slightly out of reach, like a landing page, a tiny API or a calculator, and let it pull you through the material.

When every topic has a reason to exist, it sticks. You stop asking “will I ever use this?” because you’re already using it.

## Learn just enough, then build

Read or watch until you can take the first step, then stop and start. You’ll never feel completely ready, and that’s fine.

Ten minutes of documentation followed by twenty minutes of building teaches more than an hour of tutorials on their own.

## Shorten the feedback loop

Change one thing, run it, look at the result. The faster you can see whether something worked, the faster your mental model corrects itself.

Work in small steps, keep the browser or terminal open next to your editor, and prefer tools that show results instantly.

:::tip
Next time you’re stuck, write one sentence: “I expected X, but got Y.” Often the sentence alone points at the bug.
:::

## Recall instead of re-reading

Re-reading feels productive because it feels familiar, but familiar isn’t the same as remembered. Close the tab and rebuild what you just learned from scratch.

Explain it out loud as if to a friend. Wherever you get stuck is exactly what to study next.

## Space it out

Come back to the same idea after a day, then a week, then a month. Each return is short, and each one makes the memory sturdier.

A few minutes of review spread over weeks beats one long cram session.

## Break things on purpose

Delete a line, rename a variable or change a value, and predict what will happen before you run it. Then check.

Understanding how something fails is one of the quickest ways to understand how it works.

## Write it down and teach it

Keep short notes in your own words, and try to teach what you learned: a blog post, a message to a classmate, a comment in the code.

If you can’t explain it simply, you’ve just found the gap.

## Protect your focus

Work in focused blocks of about 25 to 45 minutes with a short break between them, and silence notifications while you do.

Sleep matters more than one more late-night hour. A lot of learning settles while you rest.

## A simple weekly loop {-}

If you want something concrete to start with, run this loop once a week.

:::steps
1. **Plan**: Choose one small thing to build, and note what you’ll need to learn to build it.
2. **Build**: Work in short blocks. Write down every place you get stuck. Those are your next topics.
3. **Review**: Recall it from memory, note what you learned in your own words, then choose the next thing.
:::

## Key takeaways {-}

- Spend less time taking things in and more time trying them.
- Start from a project, and learn only what the next step needs.
- Keep the loop short: change, run, look, adjust.
- Test yourself from memory, and come back to it after a day, a week and a month.
- Explain it to someone else, or to a blank page.

Learning fast is mostly learning deliberately. Pick a project this week, keep the loop short, and let the questions you run into decide what you study next.
